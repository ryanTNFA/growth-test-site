How The Growth Test works

Growth diagnostics, applied to public data for 217 economies, with every rule, number and document open.

About this prototype

What it is, and what it is not

The Growth Test applies growth diagnostics, the method of Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco, to public cross-country data for 217 economies. For each country it says which standard explanations for weak private investment the public evidence argues against, which deserve a closer look, and which it cannot settle, with what would settle them.

What it is not, and what a prototype means

It is a triage tool built by The New Foreign Assistance (TNFA). It does not estimate growth effects, rank countries or projects, or recommend funding. A rating of “worth a closer look” means look here, not “this binds”; only field work, microdata and judgment can establish that.

It is a prototype. Its ratings are uncalibrated. Most areas in most countries are “can’t tell”, because public cross-country data cannot settle them; every such rating says why.
The New Foreign Assistance’s view

Why this way of choosing

Growth is the only proven way out of extreme poverty, and in any economy a few problems hold private investment back far more than the rest. Growth diagnostics is the discipline of finding those few before spending. Its authors put the case plainly: reforms are not free, governments cannot do everything at once, and a reform that leaves the binding constraint in place does little for growth however sound it is on its own (Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco, “Growth Diagnostics”, 2005). Rodrik made the same point about the long reform lists of the 1990s: the same policy goals can be met by many institutional arrangements, so the question is what binds here, not what the list says (One Economics, Many Recipes, 2007).

The practical consequence for assistance is the one this site is built around. Programs that ignore what actually holds growth back in a country are unlikely to reduce poverty durably, whatever else they achieve. Choosing before spending means diagnosing first, with the government and the firms that live with the constraint, and measuring what the diagnosis could not settle. Published constraints analyses, among them those of the Millennium Challenge Corporation and the Harvard Growth Lab, show the method at full strength: months of fieldwork, microdata and judgement. Their experience also shows its limits: a diagnosis can drift toward what an agency can fund, and the link from diagnosis to the projects finally chosen has often been loose (Hausmann, Klinger and Wagner, “Doing Growth Diagnostics in Practice: A ‘Mindbook’”, 2008, on the practice; evaluations of the analyses on the drift).

The Growth Test takes the first step, the diagnosis, and makes a quick, public first read of it available for any country. An automated read from public data is a large simplification of the full exercise. A diagnosis still needs people on the ground; the aim is that they start with sharper questions.

The New Foreign Assistance holds that sustained growth is the only proven cure for extreme poverty, that aid should strengthen the public goods that let private enterprise flourish, and that programs should end when their job is done. The ratings on this site follow the method and the data, not these views. thenewforeignassistance.org

Evidence settings

Three readings: Cautious, Standard and Exploratory

Every country page can show three readings of the same evidence. They show how much a rating depends on judgment calls about how much evidence is enough.

  • Standard is the default, and the only reading to cite.
  • Cautious needs more before it rates anything: three independent measures instead of two, and stronger scores either way.
  • Exploratory makes it easier to flag an area for a closer look. It never makes it easier to say “probably not” or “ruled out”, because wrongly ruling out a real constraint costs more than taking an extra look.

Because of that, a rating can move between “can’t tell” and a verdict as the reading changes, but never from “worth a closer look” to “probably not”. Every rating carries one mark: holds under every reading, depends on the reading, or depends on the comparison countries. Click or tap the mark to see the rating under each reading.

How a rating adds up

From a measure’s gap to a rating, on one axis

Indonesia’s electricity, read from its data file. The same figure sits in every area’s card.

Every measure is compared with what an economy at the same income would show, in standard deviations, and ranked among the comparison countries; the two readings become a signal from −2 to +2, binding to the right. Each test adds up its counted measures, thicker ones counting more; the four tests add up into the net score, the price test counting double; the net score is read against the lines. A quantity or an opinion is shown but not counted on the price and camels tests, because a low quantity can mean low demand rather than scarcity.
