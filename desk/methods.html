How The Growth Test works

Growth diagnostics, applied to public data for 217 economies, with every rule, number and document open.

The three answers

Three ratings, and what each one licenses

Every area in every economy gets one of three ratings. Each is a first read of public data, not a finding, and none recommends funding.

  • Worth a closer look

    It says: the evidence leans toward this area holding private investment back here.

    It licenses: looking here first, with field work, microdata and the firms that live with it. It does not say the area binds. The crimson, likely a main constraint, needs strong evidence on at least three tests and from at least five independent sources, a high price among them, that holds across the comparison countries. It is still an instruction to look, not a finding.

  • The data argue against it

    It says: the evidence leans against this area being what holds private investment back now.

    It licenses: asking a program aimed at this area what it knows that the public data do not. This is the tool’s strongest use: it is licensed to say stop, not go. The lighter shade, probably not, has the same low bar as “worth a closer look”, so on its own it should not close a debate or drop a program. The darker, ruled out, needs much more: a low price, firms not working around it, five independent sources, three agreeing tests, recent evidence, and the same answer under every comparison set. Even then it means not the main obstacle now, not that the area is fine.

  • Unclear, in three kinds

    It says: the public data cannot answer the question for this area here. It is not an average or a neutral score.

    It licenses: nothing either way. Each kind says what would settle it.

    • Evidence too weak. Evidence exists but points nowhere clearly, or only one kind of evidence speaks. A second kind (a price, a response to change, or firms working around it) would settle it.
    • Conflicting. Some tests point one way and some the other, which often means it binds for some sectors or regions and not others. Work on those sectors or regions would settle it.
    • Data gap. Too little usable, recent evidence. A newer survey or data year would settle it, or a measure the tool does not connect yet.

In the Analyst View and in every export the ratings carry the engine’s five names: likely a main constraint, worth a closer look, unclear (in its three kinds), probably not the main constraint, and ruled out. The rules behind each are in the Methodology Note, section 4.8.

About this Beta

What it is, and what it is not

The Growth Test applies growth diagnostics, the method of Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco, to public cross-country data for 217 economies. For each country it says which standard explanations for weak private investment the public evidence argues against, which deserve a closer look, and which it cannot settle, with what would settle them.

What it is not, and what Beta means

It is a triage tool built by The New Foreign Assistance (TNFA). It does not estimate growth effects, rank countries or projects, or recommend funding. A rating of “worth a closer look” means look here, not “this binds”; only field work, microdata and judgment can establish that.

It is a Beta. Its ratings are uncalibrated and may be wrong. The method is strongest where prices exist (finance, skills, power, transport) and weaker for risk and policy areas, where true prices are scarce. Most areas in most countries are “can’t tell”, because public cross-country data cannot settle them; every such rating says why. Every card asks whether its rating looks right, and the Feedback button on every page takes a note or a screenshot: what you send goes to The New Foreign Assistance and nowhere else.
The New Foreign Assistance’s view

Why this way of choosing

Growth is the only proven way out of extreme poverty, and in any economy a few problems hold private investment back far more than the rest. Growth diagnostics is the discipline of finding those few before spending. Its authors put the case plainly: reforms are not free, governments cannot do everything at once, and a reform that leaves the binding constraint in place does little for growth however sound it is on its own (Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco, “Growth Diagnostics”, 2005). Rodrik made the same point about the long reform lists of the 1990s: the same policy goals can be met by many institutional arrangements, so the question is what binds here, not what the list says (One Economics, Many Recipes, 2007).

The practical consequence for assistance is the one this site is built around. Programs that ignore what actually holds growth back in a country are unlikely to reduce poverty durably, whatever else they achieve. Choosing before spending means diagnosing first, with the government and the firms that live with the constraint, and measuring what the diagnosis could not settle. Published constraints analyses, among them those of the Millennium Challenge Corporation and the Harvard Growth Lab, show the method at full strength: months of fieldwork, microdata and judgment. Their experience also shows its limits: a diagnosis can drift toward what an agency can fund, and the link from diagnosis to the projects finally chosen has often been loose (Hausmann, Klinger and Wagner, “Doing Growth Diagnostics in Practice: A ‘Mindbook’”, 2008, on the practice; evaluations of the analyses on the drift).

The Growth Test takes the first step, the diagnosis, and makes a quick, public first read of it available for any country. An automated read from public data is a large simplification of the full exercise. A diagnosis still needs people on the ground; the aim is that they start with sharper questions.

The New Foreign Assistance holds that sustained growth is the only proven cure for extreme poverty, that aid should strengthen the public goods that let private enterprise flourish, and that programs should end when their job is done. The ratings on this site follow the method and the data, not these views. thenewforeignassistance.org

Evidence settings

Three readings: Cautious, Standard and Exploratory

Every country page can show three readings of the same evidence. They show how much a rating depends on judgment calls about how much evidence is enough.

  • Standard is the default, and the only reading to cite.
  • Cautious needs more before it rates anything: three independent measures instead of two, and stronger scores either way.
  • Exploratory makes it easier to flag an area for a closer look. It never makes it easier to say “probably not” or “ruled out”, because wrongly ruling out a real constraint costs more than taking an extra look.

Because of that, a rating can move between “can’t tell” and a verdict as the reading changes, but never from “worth a closer look” to “probably not”. Every rating carries one mark: holds under every reading, depends on the reading, or depends on the comparison countries. Click or tap the mark to see the rating under each reading.

The question the tests ask

If this were binding, now, in this country, what would we expect to see?

Every area is examined the same way. Suppose it were what holds private investment back, now, in this economy: what would the public data show? The four tests are four ways of looking, and the reading rests on the pattern they make together, never on one test alone.

  1. Is it expensive or rationed? The price of the constraint, or the queue for it.
  2. When it eases, does investment respond? Movement over time, not a level.
  3. Do firms pay to work around it? Generators, own wells, informal credit, private security.
  4. Do the activities that need it most do worst? Camels thrive where water is scarce; hippos do not.
  5. Is it unusually weak for an economy like this? Benchmarking, a fifth way of looking, which never counts. It is read two ways: against similar economies, as the grid's first column shows, and against the country's own other areas, since a poor economy is behind on nearly everything; the grid ranks each area's gap within the country. A weak area is not, for that reason, a binding one.

The complexity approach, the product space, is a lens the self-discovery page shows, not a test the tool scores. The discovery gap drawn from it is one measure among that area's evidence.

The four tests, one at a time

What each test reads, and what counts

Every measure is first compared with what an economy at the same income would show, since almost everything improves as countries get richer.

1. Is it expensive or rationed?

If an area binds, its price is high, or firms cannot get it at the going price. The test reads prices and firms’ own experience of rationing: the real lending rate, returns to schooling, outages in a typical month, loans refused, bribes asked for. Quantities, such as credit to GDP or school enrollment, are shown and do not count, because a low quantity can mean low demand rather than scarcity. This test counts double in the net score.

2. When it eases, does investment respond?

If high interest rates hold investment back, investment should fall when rates rise and recover when they fall. The test reads movement over time, not a level: whether the two have moved together over the last 20 years more often than chance would give.

3. Do firms pay to work around it?

Firms that buy generators, finance investment from their own savings, hire private security or stay informal are paying to get around a constraint. These count because they cost money and pay off only if the constraint binds. A quantity or a stated reason filed under this test, such as firms saying they need no loan, is shown and does not count.

4. Do the activities that need it most do worst? Camels, hippos and θ

In a desert the camels thrive and the hippos do not. If finance is scarce, an economy should export unusually little of what needs a lot of finance, and its firms in finance-hungry sectors should grow more slowly.

What θ asks. Does the economy’s export basket lean away from products that need the factor, compared with economies at the same income? θ (theta) is that lean: one number per economy and factor, estimated across the products it exports. What all economies share, and what rises or falls with income, is taken out first, so θ measures only what is unusual for an economy this rich.

How to read its sign. A negative θ means fewer factor-heavy products than income predicts: more camels, which points toward the factor binding. A positive θ points away. A θ near zero says nothing either way. The hippo gap (γ, gamma) asks the presence question instead: is the economy missing altogether from factor-heavy products? It is positive when it is, and absence has other causes too: comparative advantage, market size, or that nobody has tried yet.

Two checks, for two different risks.

  • The shuffle test, on each trade measure. The factor’s intensity values are reassigned at random among industries 99 times, which keeps the structure of trade and removes the economics. A measure counts only if its real tilts are steadier over five years, and agree better across two ways of measuring exports, than 95 percent of the shuffled versions. It asks whether the measure picks up the factor at all, or only the shape of trade.
  • The two-standard-error check, on each economy. Where a measure counts, an economy’s θ moves its rating only if it lies more than two standard errors from zero. It keeps a tilt that could be noise from moving a rating. A tilt that clears it scores by where it falls among all economies, from +2 for the strongest tenth of camels to −2 for the strongest tenth the other way.

Which versions count today. Twelve firm-level versions, from the World Bank’s Enterprise Surveys, ask whether firms in the sectors most exposed to a constraint grow more slowly; all twelve count, each having passed a power check that recovers a planted tilt of known size. Of six export tilts from trade data, only skills counts, toward human capital. Finance, collateral-poor products, contract intensity, transport time and electricity did not pass the shuffle test, so they are drawn on the area’s chart and do not count. Planted-tilt checks suggest why: a tilt of realistic size is hard to recover from trade data. Of the hippo gaps, contract intensity counts, toward property rights; the rest are shown only. Every area page says which of its tilts are shown only.

Borrowed: the test itself, from Hausmann, Klinger and Wagner’s Mindbook; its design, from Rajan and Zingales (1998); the published intensity measures (Rajan and Zingales; Ciccone and Papaioannou; Nunn; Braun; Hummels and Schaur; the US Bureau of Economic Analysis); the trade data, from the Harvard Growth Lab; and the firm surveys, from the World Bank. Added by the tool: one tilt per economy and factor, measured against economies at the same income, with the shuffle test on each measure and the two-standard-error check on each economy.

How a rating adds up

From a measure’s gap to a rating, on one axis

Indonesia’s electricity, read from its data file. The same figure sits in every area’s card.

Every measure is compared with what an economy at the same income would show, in standard deviations, and ranked among the comparison countries; the two readings become a signal from −2 to +2, binding to the right. Each test adds up its counted measures, thicker ones counting more and overlapping ones discounted, so measures that agree strengthen the test and a neutral one weakens it; the four tests are averaged into the net score, the price test counting double; the net score is read against the lines. A quantity or an opinion is shown only on the price and camels tests, because a low quantity can mean low demand rather than scarcity.

Known limits

What the method cannot do, and what the tool does about it

Felipe, Usui and Abdon (2011) set out the main criticisms of growth diagnostics from practice. Each, and the tool's answer:

  • Price and non-price signals are hard to tell apart. A high price can signal scarcity, or a market that works. The tool sorts every measure by what it reads (a price, a workaround, a quantity, an index), and on most tests only prices, rationing and costly behavior count: the evidence classes.
  • The branches of the tree are not independent. One problem can show in several areas. The tool asks for more than one kind of evidence before it flags an area, and lets recent measures that are shown only open a closer look only when they agree and none disagrees: the corroboration rule. An area with one kind of evidence says so.
  • The method is static: it reads what binds today. So does the tool. Every measure carries its year, and every reading is a first read of public data to a date, not a forecast.
  • It was built more for starting growth than for keeping it going. The overview names the transition the reading is about, from the growth record (getting out of a collapse, speeding up, keeping an acceleration going), and says that what binds one transition need not bind another.
  • It assumes that investment drives growth. The tool states that premise once, on the standard's page: a binding constraint is one whose easing moves private investment and entrepreneurship, the margin on which growth speeds up.
