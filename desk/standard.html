Growth-Grade Evidence

Evidence is growth-grade when it shows, in order, what binds, that it can be moved, and that this program moves it cost-effectively.

Why a standard

Over twenty years, randomized trials have taught the field a great deal about what moves a village-level outcome. They cannot, by their nature, tell a government or a donor what holds back a national economy, and the field has let “evidence-based” come to mean “measured well” rather than “on the path to growth”. This is not an argument against trials. It is an argument about what they show. A trial can establish that an intervention moves an outcome. Whether that outcome is on the path to growth in that economy is a different question, and it comes first.

A well-run trial that moves an outcome nobody has connected to growth is evidence about measurement. It is not yet evidence about poverty.

The three questions

Ask them of anything proposed, in order. They start from the problem, not the program: a program looking for a problem to solve is where most proposals begin, and where the chain breaks.

  1. What binds?Of the many things wrong, which few hold private investment back here? Answered by diagnosis: the tool gives a first read, and the country confirms it.
  2. Can it be moved?Why it binds, and whether a lever within reach (a policy, an institution, an investment) would ease it at a scale that matters. Answered by root-cause work in the country.
  3. Does this move it cost-effectively?Evidence that the program eases the constraint, measured on the constraint and not on the program’s own outputs, at a cost the gain justifies. Answered by evaluation and cost-benefit analysis; this is where trials belong.

The premise behind the questions is stated once, here, and not asked of each program: a binding constraint is one whose easing moves private investment and entrepreneurship, the margin on which growth speeds up. The chain is the order the Millennium Challenge Corporation already follows: a constraints analysis, then the root causes, then projects that must clear an economic rate of return.

What the tool speaks to

The first question, from public data, as a first read

The Growth Test rates, for 217 economies, which of a dozen standard constraints the public data argue against, which deserve a closer look, and which no one can settle yet, with the reason and what would settle it. That is a first read on the first question only. It never answers the second or the third, so it never recommends funding. It says “unclear” more often than anything else, because public data arrive with a lag and cross-country evidence cannot settle most questions about most countries. The live insight still comes from the country.
