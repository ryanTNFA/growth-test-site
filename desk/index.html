Getting to Growth · an initiative of The New Foreign Assistance

Most people were poor for most of history.

Incomes were flat for centuries. Then, in a few places and later in most, they rose, and the share of people in extreme poverty fell from three in four in 1820 to one in ten in 2018. That escape is not a line a country crosses and leaves behind. It is a climb, and it lasts as long as growth does.

2 · Getting to Growth

Growth did that, and nothing else has.

An economy is a system, not a project. It cannot be planned into growth; it can be unleashed, when what holds it back gives way. So ask three questions of anything proposed, in order.

  1. What binds?Of the many things wrong, which few hold private investment back here?
  2. Can it be moved?Why it binds, and whether a lever within reach would ease it at a scale that matters.
  3. Does this move it cost-effectively?Evidence that the program eases the constraint, measured on the constraint, at a cost the gain justifies.

Most aid proposals start at the third. Rigor there is welcome. Without the first two, it answers the wrong question well.

3 · Getting to Growth · question 1: what binds?

Growth is not a list.

In every economy a few things hold investment back far more than the rest. A reform that leaves them in place does little, however sound it is on its own.

Treating the symptoms of being poor is not stopping being poor. Reform recipes fail by design: the list is the same everywhere, and what binds is not.

Handel, D., forthcoming essay (Council on Foreign Relations), on treating the symptoms of being poor; Rodrik, D., One Economics, Many Recipes, 2007, on the reform lists of the 1990s.

4 · The Growth Test · a first read on what binds, for 217 economies, from public data

Finding constraints is a discipline, not a guess.

Growth diagnostics works down a tree. Is investment held back by the cost of finance, or by low returns? Then, for each candidate, four tests.

Costly finance, or low returns?
Financeinternational finance · domestic savings · bank lending
Low returnsskills · electricity · transport · ICT and water · land · macroeconomic risk · microeconomic risk · self-discovery · coordination
  1. Is it expensive or rationed?The price of the constraint, or the queue for it.
  2. When it eases, does investment respond?Movement over time, not a level.
  3. Do firms pay to work around it?Generators, own wells, informal credit.
  4. Do the activities that need it most do worst?Camels thrive where water is scarce; hippos do not.

The Growth Test runs those tests on public data for 217 economies. It gives a first read on the first question only, what binds; the country confirms it, and the second and third questions are for work in the country. For each area it gives one of three answers: the data argue against it; worth a closer look; or unclear, with the reason. Unclear is the honest answer more often than not, and it comes with what would settle it.

Fast, not final. A first read in minutes instead of months, from public data that arrive with a lag, in a world that does not wait for them. It cuts the time to the first insight. The live insight still comes from the country, and a fix that is not on what binds does not unleash growth credibly or durably.

Hausmann, R., D. Rodrik and A. Velasco, “Growth Diagnostics”, 2005. The tool never recommends funding; every rating shows its sources, its dates and the arguments against it. The method, in full →

5 · Getting to Growth · questions 2 and 3

A diagnosis is where the work starts.

Knowing what binds does not say what to do about it. Two questions are left, and each needs its own kind of evidence.

  1. 02
    Can it be moved?

    A constraint lasts for a reason: a law, a price set by decree, a monopoly, a utility that cannot cover its costs. Ask why it has lasted and who gains from it. Then ask whether a lever within reach would ease it at a scale firms would notice. That is work for the country: its officials, its firms and the people who know its politics. No dataset answers it.

  2. 03
    Does this move it cost-effectively?

    Here rigorous, independent evaluation takes its rightful place. A program aimed at what binds still has to show that it eased the constraint, measured on the constraint, at a cost the gain justifies. Trials and careful natural experiments are the best tools there are for that. Publishing the result, whatever it says, is what makes the next decision better.

Growth diagnostics and the trials movement grew out of the same disappointment with the reform lists of the 1990s. They answer different questions. Asked in order, each makes the other’s answer worth more.

Rodrik, D., “Diagnostics before Prescription”, Journal of Economic Perspectives 24(3), 2010; the four-part test for an evaluation is in the sources below.

6 · Getting to Growth · The New Foreign Assistance’s view

The corollary for assistance.

Assistance that wants to be built to end has to work toward the one thing that ends the need for it.

<3%of the old foreign-assistance budget went to growth.

Where assistance has focused on what binds, with the evidence published whatever it says, it has had a record the rest did not. The Millennium Challenge Corporation, which funds against a published constraints analysis and publishes its evaluations whatever they find, is the proof that focus works.

The questions before money moves: what binds here, can it be moved, and does this move it cost-effectively?
