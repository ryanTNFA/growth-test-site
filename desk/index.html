2 · Getting to Growth

Growth did that, and nothing else has.

Development is a change in the state of a whole system, not the sum of a thousand interventions. No one has intervened a country into development. So the question to ask of anything proposed is the one Lant Pritchett put as a four-part test:

  1. Do rich countries have more of it?
  2. Did they get more of it as they grew?
  3. Do the successes since 1950 have more of it than the failures?
  4. Does a country have more of it in its fast decades than in its slow ones?

Most things that fail that test are being evaluated with great rigour anyway: the wrong question, answered well.

The test is a pointer to the growth-diagnostics way of thinking, not a second check. Pritchett, L., “Is your impact evaluation asking questions that matter? A four-part smell test”, Center for Global Development, 2014.

3 · Getting to Growth

Growth is not a list.

In every economy a few things hold investment back far more than the rest. A reform that leaves them in place does little, however sound it is on its own.

Treating the symptoms of being poor is not stopping being poor. Reform recipes fail by design: the list is the same everywhere, and what binds is not.

Handel, D., forthcoming essay (Council on Foreign Relations), on treating the symptoms of being poor; Rodrik, D., One Economics, Many Recipes, 2007, on the reform lists of the 1990s.

4 · The Growth Test · the tool’s voice: what the public data can and cannot support

Finding them is a discipline, not a guess.

Growth diagnostics works down a tree. Is investment held back by the cost of finance, or by low returns? Then, for each candidate, four tests.

Costly finance, or low returns?
Financeinternational finance · domestic savings · bank lending
Low returnsskills · electricity · transport · ICT and water · land · macroeconomic risk · microeconomic risk · self-discovery · coordination
  1. Is it expensive or rationed?The price of the constraint, or the queue for it.
  2. When it eases, does investment respond?Movement over time, not a level.
  3. Do firms pay to work around it?Generators, own wells, informal credit.
  4. Do the activities that need it most do worst?Camels thrive where water is scarce; hippos do not.

The Growth Test runs those tests on public data for 217 economies and gives one of three answers for each area: the data argue against it; worth a closer look; or unclear, with the reason. Unclear is the honest answer more often than not, and it comes with what would settle it.

Fast, not final. A first read in minutes instead of months, from public data that arrive with a lag, in a world that does not wait for them. It cuts the time to the first insight. The live insight still comes from the country, and a fix that is not on what binds does not unleash growth credibly or durably.

Hausmann, R., D. Rodrik and A. Velasco, “Growth Diagnostics”, 2005. The tool never recommends funding; every rating shows its sources, its dates and the arguments against it. The method, in full →

5 · Getting to Growth · The New Foreign Assistance’s view

The corollary for assistance.

Assistance that wants to be built to end has to work toward the one thing that ends the need for it. Under three percent of the old foreign-assistance budget went to growth.

Where assistance has focused on what binds, with the evidence published whatever it says, it has had a record the rest did not. The Millennium Challenge Corporation, which funds against a published constraints analysis and publishes its evaluations whatever they find, is the proof that focus works.

The question before money moves: is there a reason to think this addresses what binds here?
