About the tour

The tour opens the tool’s own pages, rings the one thing each stop is about and says how to read it: the Bottom line and its three lists, the first decision, the growth problem, the four tests on one area and who votes, a chart in the Analyst View read as a surprise rather than a level, what leaning away looks like, the changes and camels tests, one strong test that flags and stops there, the three readings, the comparison countries as hypotheses, and the memo. It teaches the desk half of the method: what the public data can say before anyone goes to the country.

Rubric 0.3.1 · the stops quote the tool’s reading of Indonesia on the snapshot date, and a test checks every number they quote against the data.

What you will be able to say afterwards

  1. The question. What limits private investment in this country, now? One or a few constraints bind; relaxing the others changes little.
  2. Surprise is the unit of evidence. Each number is read against what the country’s income predicts, in standard deviations, and against comparison countries.
  3. Price before quantity. Scarcity shows as a high price or rationing. Quantities and expert indices inform and never decide.
  4. Four tests, and concordance. A binding constraint has a high price, investment moves when it moves, firms work around it, and activities that need little of it do best. Several kinds of evidence must agree.
  5. Asymmetry. Flagging is easy, clearing is hard. A clear needs a low price and no workarounds. “Probably not” is not “ruled out”.
  6. “Can’t tell” is a finding, with one of four reasons and a next step.
  7. Walk the tree top down, and treat comparators as hypotheses. The first decision is a direction read from the price of finance; a verdict that changes with the comparison set is weaker.

The stops

Each opens the page it is about, with Indonesia and the state it needs in the link. Take them in order the first time.

    Four words the pages use

    Class A, B, C, DA measures the cost of a constraint directly (a price). B is behaviour that only makes sense if it binds (a generator, self-financing). C is a quantity or a survey answer that cannot tell cause from effect; it informs and does not vote on the price test. D is an expert rating; it never decides.
    The four tests
    • Price: is it expensive or rationed?
    • Changes: did investment move with it?
    • Workarounds: do firms pay to get around it?
    • Camels: do activities that need little of it do best?
    Each scores from −2 to +2.
    The readingsStandard is the only reading to cite. Exploratory makes it easier to flag an area for a closer look, never easier to rule one out. Cautious asks for more and stronger evidence before rating anything.
    The first decisionCostly finance or low returns is the fork at the root of the tree, read as a direction (leans to finance, leans to low returns, cannot tell which side), never as “constraint or not”: both sides are constraints.

    The exit test

    After the tour: a second country, drawn from Ghana, Bangladesh and Uganda, with the verdicts hidden (Predict first on). Sixty minutes, no help. For the first decision (finance, low returns, or can't tell) and each of the twelve areas: your verdict, which tests speak, which is missing, and for every "can't tell", the next step. Then reveal, and write a line on each disagreement. A pass needs no false clear anywhere, ten of the thirteen verdicts (the first decision counts as one) matching or soundly argued, four in five explanations naming the right tests, and a first decision read from the price of finance.

    The tool is a check on your reading, not the answer: a disagreement argued in the method's terms counts. The rubric is docs/teaching-exit-rubric.md.
