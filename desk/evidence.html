How to read this page

What it is for. Each node of the growth diagnostics tree asks whether one thing (finance, skills, electricity and so on) is holding back private investment. This page shows the evidence for one node at a time. The verdict itself is on the Bottom line; this page shows what that verdict weighed, so you can check it or build your own charts.

"Toward binding" is a direction, not a conclusion. A dot on the binding side means that one indicator looks the way it would if the constraint held back growth, compared with economies at the same income level. One indicator never settles it. The tool is built to say when a constraint can be set aside, not to say a program should target one.

"sd" means standard deviations from what income (and, where stated, population, resource rents and being landlocked) predicts. Within ±0.75 sd reads as normal for an economy of its kind; beyond ±2 sd is unusual.

The four tests (the Hausmann-Rodrik-Velasco questions):

  • 1 · Price: is the thing expensive, or are firms rationed?
  • 2 · Changes: when it got cheaper or dearer, did investment follow?
  • 3 · Circumvention: are firms paying to work around it (generators, self-financing)?
  • 4 · Camels and hippos: do the activities that thrive here need little of it? Camels do well in a desert; if an economy exports few products that need, say, external finance, finance may be the desert.

Every chart can be saved as PNG, SVG or CSV, and "Download chart pack" saves every chart on the page in one file. Hollow or dashed marks are older than the staleness limit.

What the indicator classes A to D mean

Each indicator carries a letter for how directly it reveals a binding constraint (Workbook 4.6). The letter sets its weight in the verdict. Prices and rationing say the most; quantities and perceptions say less, because a low quantity can mean low demand rather than scarcity.

A verdict at either extreme must rest on at least two class A or B families.
