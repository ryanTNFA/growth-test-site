# How The Growth Test works

*A plain-language guide for program officers, Hill staff and managers. Companion to the Growth Test Methodology Note, v0.1 (rubric 0.1.7, engine 0.1.5, data snapshot 2026-10-24). The New Foreign Assistance (TNFA), 29 October 2026; updated 31 October.*

**The Growth Test is a prototype.** Its ratings are uncalibrated and may be wrong; most areas read “unclear” because public data cannot settle them. Use it to sharpen questions, not to settle them.

## 1. What question it answers

Private investment and growth can be held back by many things: expensive credit, unreliable power, poor roads, corruption, unstable prices, a shortage of skills. Economists call the one that matters most at a given time the *binding constraint*. Fixing a constraint that does not bind changes little; that is how well-meant programs fail.

The Growth Test looks at one country at a time and asks, for each possible constraint: does the public evidence already argue against it, does it point toward it, or can we not tell? It uses the growth-diagnostics decision tree of Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco, the one behind published constraints analyses, and it uses only public data (World Bank, IMF, firm surveys, trade data) for 217 economies.

It is a triage tool. It tells you where to look first and which arguments the evidence has already weakened. It does not tell you what to fund. *(Methodology Note §1)*

## 2. What each rating means, and what it does not

Each area of the tree gets one of these ratings.

| Rating | What it means | What it does **not** mean |
|---|---|---|
| **Ruled out** | Strong, recent evidence from several independent sources says this is not what is holding investment back, and the answer does not change with the choice of comparison countries. | It does not mean the area is fine, only that it is not the main obstacle now. |
| **Probably not the main constraint** | The evidence leans against this area. | It is **not** "ruled out". The bar is low, the same as for "worth a closer look" in the other direction. Do not use it to close a debate. |
| **Unclear** (three kinds) | *Data gap*: not enough usable evidence. *Conflicting*: some evidence points one way and some the other, which often means the problem binds for some sectors or regions and not others. *Evidence too weak*: evidence exists but points nowhere clearly. | It is not a neutral or average score. It is a statement that the desk data cannot answer the question. |
| **Worth a closer look** | The evidence leans toward this area being a constraint. | It does **not** mean the area is binding, and it is **not** a recommendation to fund anything. |
| **Likely a main constraint** | Strong evidence on several tests, from several sources, consistent across comparison countries. | It is still not a funding recommendation: it says the desk evidence is consistent with a binding constraint. Fieldwork has to confirm it and explain why it persists. |

The ratings are deliberately lopsided. The tool needs much more evidence to rule something out than to flag it, because wrongly ruling out the real problem is the costliest mistake it can make. That is what "licensed to say stop, not go" means: the tool's strongest use is to stop a program aimed at a constraint the evidence argues against, never to start one. *(Methodology Note §1.4 and §4.8)*

## 3. How confident to be

- **It is a prototype.** Its thresholds are starting points, and some were set after looking at results; none has been calibrated against outside judgement. Treat every rating as a starting point.
- **Most answers are "unclear".** About nine in ten ratings across all countries are a form of "unclear". Two areas (domestic savings, land) cannot be rated for any country with the data connected today; crime can be rated only where a firm survey since 2018 asked about security costs, because the newest surveys no longer do. Self-discovery is rated from its own trade evidence (the discovery gap), and policy uncertainty is described as context, not rated. Every "unclear" says why: the evidence isn't connected yet, there is no recent data, only one kind of evidence speaks, or the evidence points both ways. An "unclear" is an honest answer, not a failure.
- **Ratings depend on the comparison countries.** Each country is compared with countries of similar income, size, resource dependence and geography, and also with its region and with countries that grew fast from a similar starting point. A rating that changes a lot across these comparisons cannot reach the top or bottom rating. The page shows which countries were used.
- **Firm surveys are often several years old.** Older evidence counts for less, and a rating needs at least one price or workaround measure from the last eight years.
- **The strongest ratings are rare.** In the current run (rubric 0.3.1) four of the roughly 2,800 ratings across 217 countries reach an extreme: one is "ruled out" (business regulation in Uzbekistan) and three are "likely a main constraint" (finance in Maldives, electricity in Niger, human capital in Zambia). An earlier version had seven, all about business regulation, each resting on a count of new firms, a measure too indirect to decide an extreme rating; that route was closed. Expect "worth a closer look" and "probably not" to be the strongest answers you usually see. *(Methodology Note §4.9, §6 and §7.1)*

## 4. The four tests in plain words

For each area the tool asks the four questions that growth diagnosticians ask.

1. **Is it expensive or rationed?** If something is truly scarce, its price is high or people cannot get it at any price. High real interest rates point to scarce finance; high pay for educated workers points to scarce skills; firms paying bribes point to costly corruption. This is the most important test and counts double.
2. **When it changes, does investment respond?** If high interest rates hold investment back, investment should fall when rates rise and recover when they fall. The tool checks whether that happened more often than chance over the last 20 years.
3. **Are people paying to get around it?** Firms that buy generators, fund everything from their own savings, or stay informal are working around a constraint. Workarounds are strong evidence because they cost money.
4. **Do the activities that need it least do best?** In a desert the camels thrive and the hippos do not. If finance is scarce, the economy should export unusually little of what needs a lot of finance. Each version of this test must first beat randomly shuffled versions of itself, a placebo check, before it counts. So far only the skills version passes and counts toward the human-capital rating. The finance and transport versions are shown on the charts but do not count.

Every number is compared with what is normal for countries at a similar income level, because almost everything improves as countries get richer. A low score that is normal for a country's income level is not a warning sign. Any rating other than "unclear" needs at least two independent pieces of evidence, at least one of them a recent price or workaround measure. Quantities (how much credit, how many roads) and broad governance indices are shown as context but are never counted as evidence of a high price, because a low quantity can mean low demand rather than scarcity. *(Methodology Note §3 and §4.2–4.5)*

## 5. What it cannot see

- **Conflict and fragile settings.** Comparisons with other countries are weak guides where the main problem is insecurity, and the page says so for countries on the World Bank's fragile and conflict list.
- **Services.** The trade data cover goods only. A country whose growth is in tourism, finance or business services is partly invisible to the trade-based measures.
- **Variation within the country.** Everything is a national average. A constraint that binds in one region, one sector, or for women-owned or small firms only, may not show.
- **Anything after the data year.** Each number has a date, often one to three years before today, and firm surveys can be older. Reforms, shocks or elections since then are not in the data.
- **Coordination problems** in specific sectors (missing standards, certification, specialized logistics) need sector study; the tool marks that area "needs sector study" for every country. *(Methodology Note §7.1)*

## 6. Worked example: Kenya

*Numbers from the Kenya page (`desk/country.html?iso=KEN`), data snapshot 2026-10-24. Comparison countries: Côte d'Ivoire, Morocco, Cambodia, Tanzania, the Philippines, Bangladesh, Senegal, Pakistan, Benin and Honduras.*

**The growth question.** Kenya's income per person grew 2.5 percent a year over 2016–2025, with no single dominant pattern (no recent slowdown, collapse or unusual volatility). Investment was 17.6 percent of GDP, low for Kenya's income level (one standard deviation below what is typical).

**Bottom line.** Nothing is ruled out and nothing is "probably not". One area is worth a closer look: **corruption** (under micro-level risks). Everything else is unclear.

**Corruption: worth a closer look.** In the 2025 World Bank Enterprise Survey, 33 percent of Kenyan firms reported at least one bribe request. That is far above what is typical at Kenya's income level (3.6 standard deviations above) and the highest of Kenya and its ten comparison countries. Thirty-nine percent reported informal payments to public officials, and 25 percent said they were expected to give gifts in meetings with tax officials; both are also far above expectation.

Why only "worth a closer look" and not "likely a main constraint"?
- All of this evidence answers one question, the first test (is it costly?). There is no evidence yet on the other three: whether investment responds, how firms work around corruption, and which activities thrive.
- It rests on two independent pieces of evidence, both from the same 2025 survey. The top rating needs at least five, with three of the four tests agreeing.
- The broad governance index puts Kenya about where its income predicts. Composite indices like this are shown but do not count.

What it does not mean: it does not say corruption is *the* binding constraint in Kenya, and it does not say an anti-corruption program would raise investment. It says that firm-reported bribery is unusually high and that fieldwork should test whether it is what holds investment back.

**Finance: unclear, evidence too weak.** Two tests pull against each other, neither strongly enough to count as conflicting:
- *Toward a finance problem:* the real interest rate averaged 8.6 percent over 2022–2024, above what is typical for Kenya's income (1.1 standard deviations). The tool's first-decision reading is, provisionally, "supply of finance constrained".
- *Against:* Kenyan firms pay for 59 percent of their investment from their own funds, less than in all but one comparison country, and more Kenyan firms use bank loans for investment and working capital than in all but one comparison country. That is the opposite of the workaround pattern a finance constraint would produce.
- *Changes:* investment moved as a finance constraint would predict in 11 of 19 years, which is no better than chance.

The overall score is close to zero, so the rating is "unclear". A program premised on "Kenyan firms cannot get credit" cannot cite this tool in support, and neither can an argument that finance has been ruled out.

**Electricity shows how comparisons matter.** Against the default comparison countries, electricity is "unclear"; against countries that grew fast from a similar starting point, it is "worth a closer look". When a rating depends on the comparison, the page shows it, and the rating cannot reach the strongest levels. *(Methodology Note §3, §4 and §5)*

## 7. How to cite it

Name the rating exactly, the area, the country, and the versions:

> The Growth Test (TNFA, prototype; rubric 0.1.6, engine 0.1.4, data snapshot 2026-10-24): for Kenya, corruption is rated "worth a closer look"; 33 percent of firms reported a bribe request (Enterprise Survey 2025).

Write "the evidence leans toward" or "worth a closer look", not "is the binding constraint". Write "probably not the main constraint", not "ruled out", unless the page says "ruled out". Say that the tool is a prototype and not yet validated. Formal releases with a permanent identifier (DOI) are planned but do not exist yet. *(Methodology Note §8)*

## 8. Check your understanding

Five questions. Someone who has read this guide should answer at least four correctly.

**1. The Growth Test rates corruption in Kenya "worth a closer look". Should a donor fund an anti-corruption program in Kenya on that basis?**
No. The tool never recommends funding. The rating says firm-reported bribery is unusually high and deserves field study. It does not show that corruption is what holds investment back, or that a program would help.

**2. An area is rated "probably not the main constraint". Can you tell your manager it has been ruled out?**
No. "Probably not" has a low bar, the mirror image of "worth a closer look". "Ruled out" requires much more evidence from several independent sources and is rare.

**3. Kenya's finance rating is "unclear: evidence too weak". Does that mean finance is fine in Kenya?**
No. It means the evidence points in different directions (interest rates are high, yet firms use banks more and rely on their own funds less than in comparable countries), so the desk data cannot answer the question either way.

**4. A program targets electricity in one province of a country. Can the tool tell you whether power is the binding constraint in that province?**
No. Every number is a national average. The tool cannot see differences within a country, between sectors, or between kinds of firms.

**5. What must a citation of a Growth Test rating include?**
The country, the area, the rating's exact name, the version numbers or data snapshot date, and the fact that the tool is an unvalidated prototype. Ideally also the key number behind the rating and its year.
